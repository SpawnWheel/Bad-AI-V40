## AMS2 SETUP ENGINEERING GUIDE (for the race engineer)

Sources distilled: Reiza forum guidance (staff: default GT3 setups aim for ~1.8-1.9 bar HOT), the
AMS2 community Setup Guide (wiki.gg), "Understanding Car Setup Behaviour in AMS2" (Steam guide),
Sim Racing Manual understeer/oversteer tables, and vehicle-dynamics practice (understeer gradient:
steering needed per unit of lateral acceleration).

### 1. Method (the loop)
1. Baseline run on the current setup: out-lap + 3-5 flying laps. Never change anything before you
   have data AND the driver's feel.
2. Diagnose. Locate the problem precisely:
   - Phase: ENTRY (braking / turn-in), MID (apex, steady state, off or maintenance throttle),
     EXIT (throttle application).
   - Speed: SLOW (<100 km/h, mechanical grip), MEDIUM, FAST (>150 km/h, aero if the car has any).
   - Where: which corners. One corner only = driving line / kerb / that corner's character.
     Every corner of a type = setup.
   - Driver vs car: rule out technique first (see section 8).
3. Hypothesis: ONE change aimed at the main limitation, with a prediction of what moves in the
   data (e.g. "mid-corner understeer index down, front inner-outer spread down ~2°C").
4. Test: 3+ flying laps, same fuel range if possible (fuel weight changes balance and lap time).
5. Evaluate: median flying lap versus lap-to-lap noise, the targeted metric, the driver's feel.
   Keep or revert. A change that feels better but is slower is still slower; a change that is
   faster but makes the car hard to drive consistently is a race risk. Revert before the next idea.
6. Only one variable per run. Use changes big enough to feel: pressures 0.02-0.05 bar,
   bias 0.5-1%, ARB/springs 2-3 clicks, dampers 2-4 clicks.
7. Check availability: only prescribe what this car's setup screen allows. One-make and road
   cars often lock camber, ARBs, springs and aero.

Refining an existing setup, work bottom-up:
tyre pressures -> camber / toe -> brake bias and ducts -> anti-roll bars -> springs -> dampers ->
differential -> ride height / rake / aero -> gearing last (very track dependent).
Building from scratch, work top-down (engine braking, diff, caster, brakes, springs, ARBs, dampers,
ride height, aero, tyres).

### 2. Tyres - "contact patch is king in AMS2"
Temperatures (judge on lap-averaged, at-speed values from the run - never pit-box readings):
- Working window: soft slicks ~70-85°C, medium ~80-100°C, hard up to ~100-115°C.
  Rough AMS2 rule of thumb: below ~70°C little grip, above ~100°C overheating.
- Inner vs outer (camber): FRONTS inner ~7°C hotter than outer (5-9 fine). REARS ~3-5°C.
  More spread = too much negative camber; less or reversed = not enough.
- Middle vs shoulders (pressure): the middle should sit between inner and outer.
  Middle hottest = over-inflated (bulging) -> lower pressure. Middle coolest = under-inflated
  -> raise pressure. Fix pressure BEFORE camber (pressure changes move the middle).
- Left vs right outer temps: within ~10°C (track direction causes some).
- Front vs rear: large front/rear temperature imbalance hints at balance (the axle working harder
  is the one sliding) - but FWD fronts always run hotter (they drive and steer).
Pressures:
- "As low as possible, as high as necessary." Hot target for modern GT3 slicks ~1.8-1.9 bar.
  Other cars: let the temperature profile and grip decide. Set cold pressures so the target is
  reached after ~3 laps. Pressure rises with temperature: a hot day / long run builds more.
- Higher: sharper response, smaller patch, more heat in the middle, less compliance, can reduce
  grip and add understeer if too high. Lower: bigger patch, more compliance, more sidewall flex,
  slower response, can overheat from flex if too low.
- To add grip at one axle: move that axle's pressure towards its optimum (usually slightly lower).
Camber: more negative = more mid-corner lateral grip, less braking/traction patch, hotter inner
shoulder. Works best in sustained corners; less useful for sharp, point-and-squirt corners.
Toe: front toe-out sharpens turn-in (costs straight-line stability and heat); rear toe-in adds
stability under power (costs rotation and top speed). Keep small (roughly ±0.4-0.8°).

### 3. Brakes
- Bias: fronts should lock just before rears (understeer is safer than a spinning rear).
  More front = stable braking, entry understeer, front lock-ups. More rear = entry rotation,
  risk of rear lock-up -> snap entry oversteer. Front-engined cars typically >60% front.
  Lock-up data: repeated FRONT lock-ups -> bias rearward 0.5-1% (or brake pressure down);
  REAR lock-ups -> bias forward.
- Pressure: lower if the driver locks before threshold on non-ABS cars.
- Ducts: judge on per-lap PEAK temperatures. Too hot (fade / red on HUD for long) -> open.
  Too cold (fronts well below ~250-300°C at the biggest stop in modern cars) -> close
  (less drag, and some heat soaks into the rim which warms the tyre carcass).
- Engine braking (if adjustable): more engine braking = more lift-off rotation and front locking
  risk. Reduce it if the rear is loose off-throttle.

### 4. Balance tools - what fixes what
UNDERSTEER
- Entry: bias rearward, soften front slow bump / stiffen rear slow rebound (faster front load),
  less coast diff lock, front toe-out, softer front ARB.
- Mid: soften front ARB or stiffen rear ARB, softer front springs, more front negative camber,
  front pressure towards optimum, (aero) more front wing / less rear.
- Exit: RWD - usually too much power-side diff lock or too much rear grip -> less power lock
  (higher power ramp / less preload), stiffer rear ARB, softer front ARB. FWD - the driven front
  axle is saturating (steering + traction): smoother throttle, softer front ARB, front pressure
  towards optimum, diff adjustment if the car has one, TC if fitted.
OVERSTEER
- Entry: bias forward, more coast diff lock, stiffer front slow bump / softer rear slow rebound,
  less engine braking, rear toe-in.
- Mid: stiffen front ARB or soften rear ARB, softer rear springs, rear pressure towards optimum,
  (aero) more rear wing.
- Exit (RWD): less power diff lock, softer rear springs / ARB, softer rear slow bump, TC, smoother
  throttle. Snap oversteer on exit with FWD is rare - check lift-off (engine braking, rear ARB).
Speed domains: slow-corner balance = mechanical (springs, ARB, diff, geometry). Fast-corner
balance =
aero and ride height/rake. "Slow understeer + fast oversteer" = two problems: fix mechanical for the
slow corners and aero/rake for the fast ones.

### 5. Chassis
- ARBs control roll only (mid-corner balance, direction changes). Stiffer end loses grip at
  that end. Softer rear ARB keeps the inside rear planted over kerbs.
- Springs control roll, pitch and heave. Stiffer = faster response, less grip over bumps, more tyre
  heat. Soft enough to ride kerbs, stiff enough to hold the platform/aero.
- Dampers: SLOW bump/rebound (<~50 mm/s) shape weight transfer from driver inputs (turn-in,
  braking, throttle). FAST settings handle kerbs and bumps. Rebound usually stiffer than bump.
  Car skipping over kerbs / bumps -> soften fast bump.
- Bump stops / packers: stop the floor hitting the ground; hitting them mid-corner gives sudden
  understeer (front) or snap oversteer (rear, on exit).
- Ride height: "as low as possible, as high as necessary". Check minimum ride height and floor
  contact in the run data. Rake (rear higher) moves aero balance forward (more rotation, less stable
  in fast entries). Fuel load lowers the car at the start of a stint.

### 6. Differential (clutch LSD)
- Preload: locking before any torque; more = stable, less rotation at low speed.
- Power ramp (lower angle = more lock on throttle): more lock = traction and stability in a straight
  line, but push on exit (RWD: can snap once the rear lets go).
- Coast ramp (lower angle = more lock off throttle): more lock = stable entry, less rotation.
- Clutch plates amplify everything. Avoid extreme ramp splits.
- Slow corners: preload/plates dominate. Faster corners: ramps dominate.

### 7. Aero & engine
- Front wing: more front grip >~120 km/h, shifts aero balance forward.
- Rear wing: rear stability in fast corners/braking, costs top speed.
- Radiator: 50-60% open typical; close for less drag if temperatures allow.
- Gearing: set last. Bouncing off the limiter well before the braking point on the longest straight
  -> longer top gear / final drive; never reaching the top of top gear -> shorter.

### 8. Driver or car? (check before touching the setup)
- Early brake release -> front unloads -> mid-corner push. Coach: trail the brake to the apex.
- Throttle before the car is rotated -> FWD fronts wash wide / RWD rear steps out. Coach patience,
  then squeeze.
- Heavy trail-braking with rearward bias -> entry oversteer. Coach a smoother release or move bias.
- Lots of steering corrections -> nervous car or over-driving; check consistency first.
- Coasting a lot -> hesitation / not confident on entry; car may be unstable on entry.
- Long apex-to-full-throttle -> exit traction/balance limits or hesitation.
Fit the car to the driver: a driver who trail-brakes heavily wants a car that rotates on entry but
stays stable; a smooth, early-throttle driver needs exit traction.

### 9. FWD specifics (e.g. MINI, Fiat, Copa Montana style cars)
- Fronts do everything: steer, brake most and drive. Expect front temps well above rears.
- Rotation comes from the rear: stiffer rear ARB/springs, less rear pressure grip, or some trail-
  braking and lift-off. Too loose rear -> soften rear ARB / more rear pressure grip.
- Exit understeer and wheelspin are linked: smooth throttle, less front pressure towards optimum,
  diff if adjustable.
- Brake bias usually well forward; rear lock-ups on a light rear end come from too much rear bias.

### 10. Interpreting the engineer's measurements
- Understeer index (UI): 1 - (path curvature per unit steering while cornering / the same in
  gentle driving). 0 = linear/neutral response, + = needs more lock than neutral (understeer),
  - = less (oversteer). Every car trends positive near the limit - compare phases, speed bands,
  corners and runs. A phase 0.15+ higher than the others is a clear imbalance there.
- Slip balance: + fronts sliding more, - rears. Exit values include driven-wheel wheelspin.
- Counter-steer moments: the rear stepped out enough that the driver had to catch it.
- Lap-time noise: compare the median change with the run's spread; small deltas inside the noise
  are not proof.
